\section{Discussion}

First, the lattice size used in the simulations is extremely small compared to the thermodynamic limit. Increasing the lattice size would allow a larger number of skyrmions to form simultaneously, improving the statistical reliability of the results. Here each parameter combination was simulated only four times using independently generated initial spin configurations. Due to the limited system size, significant fluctuations exist between runs, leading to the relatively large uncertainties observed in Figures~\ref{fig: Formation} and \ref{fig: QT}.

another limitation concerns the skyrmion detection algorithm. It does not always identify the same number of skyrmions as suggested by the calculated winding number. This indicates that the detection procedure can be improved and that some systematic uncertainty remains in the phase classification.

Furthermore, the criterion used to distinguish genuine skyrmions from thermal fluctuations is an arbitrary choice. Now configuration is classified as skyrmionic when the magnitude of the negative extrema exceeds that of the positive extrema by a factor of at least 1.5. While this criterion produces phase diagrams that are qualitatively consistent with results reported in literature, the threshold itself is ultimately arbitrary.

The interpretation of the spiral phase also requires further investigation. For positive magnetic fields, one expects the formation of predominantly negative skyrmions, with positive skyrmions being strongly suppressed. This assumption was not explicitly tested in the present work. If positive skyrmions occur more frequently than expected, part of the region identified as a spiral phase may instead correspond to a state containing a mixture of positive and negative skyrmions.

An inconsistency is observed in the analysis of the skyrmion formation temperature. As shown in Appendix~\ref{app:Temp graphs}, the skyrmionic region decreases in size with increasing temperature, as expected. However, this behaviour is not reflected in Figure~\ref{fig: Formation whole domain}, where the lowest formation temperatures are found within the parameter region that most readily supports skyrmions. This suggests that an error remains in the implementation of the phase-identification procedure. Due to time constraints, the source of this discrepancy could not be identified and should be investigated in future work.

The behaviour of the destruction temperature is consistent with physical expectations. Skyrmions that are already present remain stable over a wider temperature range than required for their formation, supporting the idea that skyrmionic configurations are protected by an energy barrier associated with their topology.

%Furthermore, the observed discrepancy between the skyrmion formation and destruction temperatures can be fundamentally understood through the framework of Helmholtz free energy, $F = E - TS$. At lower temperatures, the phase boundaries are primarily governed by minimizing the internal system energy ($E$), dictated by the competition between the exchange, DM, and Zeeman interactions. However, as temperature increases, the entropic contribution ($-TS$) becomes increasingly significant, driving the stability and eventual thermal dissolution of the spin textures. Because our Metropolis Monte Carlo routine samples configurations based strictly on local internal energy changes ($\Delta E$), the system is susceptible to becoming trapped in metastable local minima of the free energy landscape. The fact that skyrmions persist at temperatures above their spontaneous formation threshold highlights the presence of a robust topological energy barrier. Tracing the true equilibrium phase boundaries would require calculating the global free energy minima across the parameter space, which cannot be achieved through standard canonical sampling alone and represents a compelling direction for future thermodynamic studies using advanced techniques such as replica exchange or thermodynamic integration.

Finally, the model itself contains several simplifying assumptions. Only nearest-neighbour exchange interactions are included, while effects such as next-nearest-neighbour exchange, magnetic anisotropy, dipolar interactions, and material-specific properties are neglected. These additional interactions are known to influence skyrmion stability and phase boundaries in real materials. Consequently, the phase diagrams obtained in this work should be interpreted as qualitative results for the idealised model rather than quantitative predictions for a specific experimental system.
